# Intent Statements as a General Architectural Instrument

The rule is the mechanism; the intent is the load-bearing element

## Abstract

Concord's original law design principle holds that each law must state its intent, because the intent is the load-bearing element and the specific rule is only the mechanism for achieving it. This note argues that the principle is not a rule about law. It is a general architectural instrument, arising from the fractal structure, and it is already present in Concord at five distinct scales without having been recognised as one principle.

The note records those five instances, defines intent statements against adjacent categories that they are routinely confused with, sets out the failure modes each one addresses, and specifies where intent statements should and should not be placed. It argues that this is the largest single available lever on cross-domain legibility and therefore on the architectural size ceiling, and that it is the one legibility instrument the author can apply now without requiring new participants.

## 1. The principle

The principle is short: a provision states what it is for, and separately states what it requires. The requirement is a mechanism, selected to achieve the purpose under conditions known at the time. The purpose is the durable element, because it survives translation into contexts the mechanism's author never saw.

Two consequences follow directly. A law carrying its intent can be applied to circumstances its drafters did not anticipate, because the intent travels even where the enumerated mechanism does not. And a mechanism can be replaced without losing the protection it provided, provided the replacement achieves the same intent. A rule that states only itself can do neither: it must be rewritten whenever the world moves, and no one can tell whether replacing it is safe.

The author's own account of the design decision is that each law was to state what ethical principle it derives from, rather than enumerating every permutation. The stated intent is the derivation.

## 2. Five instances of one principle

The principle appears in Concord at five scales. They were arrived at separately, which is the evidence that the principle is structural rather than a local convention.

| Scale | The instrument | Form of the intent | Failures it addresses |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Law | Each law states its intent, not only the rule | The ethical principle the law derives from | Enumerative brittleness; unjudgeable application to new circumstances |
| File retention | A label is never proof of disposability; read the substance | What the file contains that matters | Loss of unique mechanisms under disposable-looking labels |
| Corpus curation | Architecture-only retention: rationale, tests and histories need not persist; architecture and methods must | The line between what judgement needs and what explains how it was reached | Bloat; and the opposite error of dropping the reasoning with the narrative |
| Constitutional | Chapter 61 states that geometry confers no automatic authority, rather than forbidding higher authority | The reason, instead of a prohibition on the outcome | Drift toward the inherited hierarchical reading |
| Governance mechanism | Anti-pooling preserved as an end, while the surrounding voting mechanism stays replaceable | No manufactured authority through expansion | Replacement of a load-bearing protection by a mechanism that looks equivalent |

Reading the table down the third column gives the same instruction each time: state what the thing is for, so that a reader who holds only the thing can judge it. Reading the fourth column down gives the same warning each time: without the intent, the artefact is judged by its surface, and its surface does not distinguish it from a local convenience.

## 3. What an intent statement is not

The instrument is weakened whenever it is confused with material that surrounds it. The distinction matters because the 1.4a architecture-only correction already rules out most of that material from the living corpus, and an intent statement that smuggled it back in would defeat the correction.

| Category | Question it answers | Status |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Intent | What is this for; what does it protect? | In the operational text |
| Rule | What is required or forbidden? | In the operational text |
| Mechanism | How is the intent achieved here? | In the operational text, replaceable |
| Rationale | Why was this mechanism chosen over others? | Archive |
| Evidence | What showed that it works? | Archive |
| Commentary | How has this developed, and who changed what? | Archive |
| Status label | Is this canonical, provisional, verified? | Removed; Concord is always current and always developing |

The last row is a distinct point. Concord's authorial position is that operational documents carry no provisional or canonical labels, because the architecture is continuously operational and continuously developing. An intent statement is not a status claim. It says what the provision is for, not how confident anyone is in it.

## 4. What intent statements do

Each of the failures identified across the companion notes is addressed by this one instrument:

Transmission failure (Note 003). a provision carrying its intent cannot be mistaken for a local convenience, because the intent declares its reach.

Reconstruction limit (Note 002). a reader holding only part of the architecture can determine what the part requires, without holding the whole.

Loss of fundamental architecture (Note 001). a removal cannot proceed without answering the intent, which is the inverse removal burden of Note 001 in its practical form.

Evolvability. the architecture stays replaceable, because the mechanism is visibly subordinate to the purpose. This is what allows Note 001's recommendation against entrenchment to be followed safely.

Turnover compression (Note 003). the reason for a provision survives a handover even when the working set does not, which is where intent is currently lost first.

## 5. Where intent statements belong

The placement rule is co-location. The intent statement belongs with the provision it explains, in the operational document, not in a separate register, rationale document or development note.

The reason is the same reason the failure occurs. A register of reasons held separately from the rules reintroduces precisely the separation that transmission failure exploits: a later reader can have the rule without reaching the register, and nothing in the rule indicates that the register matters. Co-location means that a reader who cannot reach the reason cannot reach the rule either, which converts a silent failure into a visible one.

Four placement constraints follow:

the intent statement sits adjacent to the provision, not in a preamble, appendix or companion document.

it is short enough that its loss at handover would be noticed alongside the rule's loss.

it is written in the present tense as operative content, not as historical explanation.

if a provision has no intent that survives being stated, that is itself a finding: it is either not a provision, or its intent has already been lost and belongs at the top of the recovery queue (Note 004, section 7).

## 6. Where intent statements should not go

The instrument should not be applied indiscriminately, for two reasons. Volume dilutes the signal, and a mechanical obligation to state an intent for every sentence produces intents that are restatements of the rule.

Non-provisions. descriptive, orientation and explanatory passages carry no intent statement, because they require nothing.

Restatements. a provision that restates another domain's provision takes a dependency declaration pointing at the owner, not a second intent statement. Duplicated intents drift apart.

Circular intents. an intent statement that says the same thing as the rule adds nothing and should be removed. The test is whether the statement permits judgement the rule alone does not.

Multiple provisions, one intent. where two provisions serve one intent, the intent is stated once and both provisions reference it. Repeating it produces the divergence the principle exists to prevent.

## 7. Relationship to the reason-recovery record

The two instruments are related but not identical, and the distinction should be kept. The intent statement is a standing, minimal, in-place sentence that every load-bearing provision carries. The reason-recovery record of Development Note 004 is a fuller instrument — provision, intent, failure if absent, replacement requirement and dependency — applied systematically as a recovery programme.

In practice the intent statement is the I field of the reason-recovery record, extracted and placed where the provision lives. Where a full record exists, the intent statement is its visible part. Where no full record exists yet, the intent statement can be written first, which is why the work order in Note 004 begins where it does.

## 8. Risks and counterarguments

### 8.1 Stated intent becomes an orthodoxy that blocks improvement

A recorded purpose can be treated as a commitment to the current mechanism. Mitigation: the intent must be stated as an end, never as a means, and the replacement requirement should accompany any fundamental provision so that substitution is visibly contemplated.

### 8.2 Intents can be wrong

A stated intent may not be the intent the provision actually serves, particularly where it is reconstructed. Mitigation: mark reconstructed intents as reconstructed (Note 004), and treat a wrong stated intent as preferable to an absent one, because it can be corrected while an absent one cannot be examined.

### 8.3 Increased volume raises the reading burden

More text in operational documents works against legibility. Mitigation: the instrument is one sentence per provision, and it displaces the developmental material the 1.4a correction removes. Applied as intended, the net change in operational document length should be small.

### 8.4 The principle is obvious and therefore not worth recording

The principle is obvious. Concord nevertheless lost the triadic architecture and the GTP distinction, and in both cases the failure was the absence of exactly this. The value of recording it is not that it is a discovery, but that it becomes an obligation with a test rather than a practice that everyone assumes is happening.

## 9. Recommendations

Adopt the intent statement as a standing general instrument at every architectural scale, not as a law-domain practice.

Require an intent statement for every load-bearing provision, co-located with the provision.

Require intents to be stated as ends, not as restatements of the rule or descriptions of the mechanism.

Prohibit separate intent registers; where a fuller record is needed, apply Development Note 004's method instead.

Exempt non-provisions, restatements and duplicate intents explicitly, so the obligation does not produce filler.

Treat a load-bearing provision that has no statable intent as an immediate recovery candidate, since its intent has already been lost.

Recognise the five existing instances recorded in section 2 as instances of this one principle, and cite them as precedent when extending it.

## 10. Summary

The law design principle — state the intent, because the rule is only the mechanism — is a general architectural instrument arising from the fractal structure. It appears already in Concord at five scales, arrived at independently in each case.

It is the largest single lever on cross-domain legibility, and therefore on the architectural size ceiling; it is the practical form of the reversed removal burden; and it is the instrument that keeps the architecture replaceable while protecting what the architecture protects. Placement is co-location, form is one sentence per provision, and the test is whether it permits judgement the rule alone does not.
